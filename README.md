# Predictive Modeling and Clustering of Vehicle Crashes in Iowa

Machine learning analysis of Iowa vehicle crash data (2014–2024) to predict crash severity and uncover behavioral crash patterns, in support of data-driven transportation safety insights.

**Author:** Anirudh Rajanna
**Tools:** Python (pandas, scikit-learn, matplotlib), Power BI

## Overview

This project transforms a large, messy public crash dataset into a modeling-ready foundation, then applies supervised and unsupervised machine learning to answer two questions: *what conditions predict a severe crash*, and *what distinct types of crashes exist in the data*.

The work proceeds in three stages: data preparation and exploration, predictive modeling of crash severity, and contextual clustering. An interactive Power BI dashboard presents the results by county, environment, road type, and crash severity.

## Data

- **Source:** Iowa Department of Transportation Open Data Portal, Vehicle Crashes (2014–2024)
- **Scale:** ~619,000 raw crash records across 80+ attributes, cleaned to ~590,000 modeling-ready records
- **Preparation:** column standardization, datetime parsing (year/month/weekday/hour), property-damage normalization capped at the 99th percentile, latitude/longitude extraction from geometry fields, invalid-coordinate handling, and KNN imputation for missing city/county values

## Predictive Modeling

Two algorithms, Logistic Regression and Random Forest, were trained on both a binary task (severe vs. non-severe) and a five-class severity task.

- Both models reached strong discriminative power, with **ROC-AUC > 0.93**
- Logistic Regression favored **recall** on severe crashes; Random Forest favored **precision** and overall accuracy
- On the five-class task, Logistic Regression edged ahead on **Macro-F1 (≈0.64 vs ≈0.62)**
- Threshold tuning was used on the binary task to balance precision and recall for the rare severe-crash class
- Top predictors: **lighting, weather, hour of day, and roadway context**

## Contextual Clustering

MiniBatch K-Means (chosen for scalability on ~600k rows) segmented crashes by weather, light, surface, roadway type, hour, and occupant counts. Optimal cluster count was assessed with Elbow and Silhouette methods.

- **k = 2** was the analytical optimum (Silhouette ≈ 0.177)
- A **k = 4** model was also generated for Power BI visualization
- The two dominant segments: lower-impact, low-occupant crashes vs. higher-severity, higher-occupant crashes, notably under similar clear/daylight/dry conditions, suggesting exposure and behavior, not weather, drive severity

## Limitations

Documented honestly rather than glossed over:

- **Class imbalance:** severe crashes are ~2.7% of records, which limits precision and makes severity prediction sensitive to threshold choice
- **Property-damage field** was fully missing after filtering to complete years and could not be modeled
- **Clustering is behavioral, not spatial:** it captures similarity in conditions, not geographic hotspots (which would need GIS methods)
- **No external covariates:** traffic volume, work-zone activity, enforcement, and weather-station data were not incorporated, limiting causal interpretation
